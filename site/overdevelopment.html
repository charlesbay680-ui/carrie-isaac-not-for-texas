Carrie Loves Over­development

01
Since 2001

The arranged marriage

The groundwater district under Wimberley can't levy taxes or production fees. Its main income is one-time fees on new wells and connections. So its budget only grows when more straws go into the aquifer. In 2025, the one bill that would have changed that passed both chambers. She fought it. The governor vetoed it.

02
2025 session

The one law she passed

Her HB 2817 became SB 863. It lets utilities whose service areas straddle the Edwards Aquifer Authority line use Edwards water across the whole area, including the part outside the Authority's jurisdiction. The bill's own analysis says those utilities had been inadvertently violating state law for over three decades. Her campaign's version: it helps utilities "responsibly serve areas near, but outside, EAA boundaries."

He'd been sneaking out for thirty years. She made it official.

03
2025 session

The one time she almost said no

In fairness: she filed HB 2265, which would have given Comal County land-use authority around Canyon Lake and the Guadalupe River. Counties in Texas have almost none. It died in committee. Every Hallmark heroine gets one moment at the window where she almost calls it off.

04
2025 – 2026

He keeps adding rooms

Remember Aqua from episode one? TESPA says it's adding new homes as customers around Wimberley, with plans for hundreds more, while its Woodcreek II wells ran over their legal limit. In Comal, a commissioner says the recent extreme growth rates have put a lot of pressure on infrastructure. Roads, jails, water. All of it.

05
June 2026

The dinner she missed

A coalition of Hill Country counties sets its legislative wish list: proof of groundwater before new subdivisions get approved, county authority over impervious cover, tougher permits for aggregate mining. It's a menu for taming the suitor. Local coverage doesn't list her at the table.

Her side of the aisle

People are moving here either way. Growth needs homes, roads and schools, and property owners have the right to build. SB 863 fixed a decades-old legal glitch without raising anyone's rates.

The aquifer's side

Growth isn't the problem; unplanned growth on a shared aquifer is. Counties asked for the tools to check water before approving subdivisions. Her one enacted water law eases service at the aquifer's edge and does nothing to limit pumping.
